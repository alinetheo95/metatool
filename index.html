II. Plant as Medium Slow Computation
Plant as Medium

A plant as a learning tool and a tool for communication.

This project is part of my larger thesis, which argues that we need to take on a more-than-human approach to design and architecture. In presenting this thesis, I received feedback saying how this work could live on in an educational way — to teach people to shift their attention and perspective on the systems and timescales of other species we co-exist with.

My thesis moves through a series of environmental problems, evaluated by a series of experiments with plants and mushrooms, and then presents speculative futures as solutions. In this supporting project, I am going to use attention as a means for education of my findings throughout the process of my thesis project. Using the process drawings I did throughout the project, I'll show evidence of this plant changing over time, and evidence that my understanding and perception of it are changing over time as well. The drawings act as attention records.

My stance on attention in this context is that we need to pay deeper attention to the processes that surround us that we cannot see at the human scale. In order to understand these processes and timescales that other species are working on, we must shift the way we practice attention.

III. Learning Through Form Slow Computation
Learning Through Form

In studying different forms of plants, mushrooms, and other species, I was able to wrap my mind around certain lines, contours, and shading that made up the subject I was attending to. Paying more attention to the subjects' form allowed me to notice things I wouldn't have simply by looking.

IV. Attempting to Comprehend the Unseen Slow Computation
Attempting to Comprehend the Unseen

After studying form, I grew curious about the unseen processes that allowed these plants and organisms to be. As someone with little to no biology experience, there was a learning curve in trying to understand these organisms on a molecular level.

There is a science to the pen-to-paper method. Committing things to memory and attempting to comprehend new subjects is always more successful when you put pen to paper.

IV. Attempting to Comprehend the Unseen — continued Slow Computation

I began to understand these subjects more deeply. Maybe I didn't fully understand the science behind how these subjects were existing, but there was a relationship that began to develop where I could now picture the molecules of a plant activating when it was watered, exposed to sun, or perhaps even surrounded by a person. That person being me.

The plant had my attention now in a way it hadn't before. What was plant logic? And how could I attempt to understand it?

V. Plant as Technology Slow Computation
Plant as Technology

In order to attempt to visualize how I suspected this plant might be reacting to me, I needed to incorporate a series of sensors, determining which environmental factors I wanted to measure, and what insights these would give me into understanding the plant more.

I began to research sensors, draw out circuits, and plan how these plants might exist in space with the technology that was trying to gain their attention.

VI. Error of the Unseen Slow Computation
Error of the Unseen

There were of course setbacks and learning curves along the way. In an attempt to understand what processes were at work in this plant, I connected it to an electrical circuit to see the current that ran through its body, how it changed when I touched it, how it changed when different environmental factors were introduced. Some days the plant would give no signal at all — as if it wasn't reacting to me. Was it a technological error, a code bug, or did the plant not care about me that day? Was I intruding on its natural state too much by prodding it and poking it, or attaching wires to it that literally left marks on its leaves?

I began to think about the definition of error. How do humans define error? How do plants define error?

VI. Error of the Unseen — continued Slow Computation

How do plants deal with things in the atmosphere that we cannot see? What we perceive as a yellow or brown leaf is a visual sign of something that is off balance in a plant's ecosystem. It may be overwatered, underwatered, getting too much or too little light. But these are not errors to the plant. The plant is simply reacting to its environmental conditions, and doing the best with what it can to survive.

I also began experimenting with mushrooms, seeing how they would react to me as well. This brought unseen errors to a whole new level. At first, I began growing these mushrooms in my apartment. As an inexperienced grower, I was unaware of the fact that the mushrooms need to be in a contained environment that fosters an idyllic humid microclimate, but also keeps the airborne spores that they give off when at a certain point in their growth cycle. Now I was concerned these spores were all over my apartment.

If I had found a mushroom growing somewhere I didn't want it to, it may have been an error for me, but to the mushroom, this is their form of reproduction.

VII. Plant as Attentional Practice Slow Computation
Plant as Attentional Practice

To pay attention to a subject is to truly learn about them — to form a relationship with them, to experience things with them, and to know them on the deep, molecular level at which they operate. Now that I was sort of engulfed in this sporey mushroom plant world, my attention shifted to them entirely.

I started to think about how to showcase these plants and mushrooms in a final exhibit. I was drawing them more often, noticing more detail, and getting better at drawing them. I wanted to arrange these plants and mushrooms in a way that I would be able to grab other people's attention — to be able to communicate with people and make them understand the ins and outs of the plants and mushrooms that I had discovered over the course of a year.

VII. Plant as Attentional Practice — continued Slow Computation

How could I arrange these plants and mushrooms in a way that they captured attention without me having to say anything at all? I also started to question my role as the human in this system. What was my role? Was I communicating something untrue on behalf of the plants?

VIII. Plant as Visual Tool Slow Computation
Plant as Visual Tool

In another plant experiment, I attempted to force heat onto a plant to collect temperature and humidity data throughout different parts of the plant. The heat was way too much for the plant, and its scorched leaves became an indicator of yet another process that I couldn't see, but that the plant was making visible to me.

I began to think about how plants provide visual cues for aspects of our environment that we may be unaware of. The plant's leaves were scorched, indicating that the temperature was too hot. The mushroom batch started becoming moldy, indicating that it came into contact with bacteria, either directly from my hands or from something floating around in the air that I also was breathing in.

VIII. Plant as Visual Tool — continued Slow Computation

When there is a lot of dust in your house, the first place you'll notice it is on your plants' leaves. If the air is too dry, mushroom caps will become so firm that they don't even move. I thought a lot about how to create a visual that would indicate the plant's communication, in a way that felt right for the plant.

IX. Conclusion Slow Computation
Conclusion

To conclude, I'm asking myself what I've learned from these plants and mushrooms. Have I communicated their message successfully? Will people rethink the way they operate the next time they come into contact with a plant?

In trying to depict what I think the plant wants to become, I've worked through a series of sketches for future scenarios in which I believe designers truly pay attention to their molecular properties and rethink how we use them in spatial design. These plants are scaled up, given new roles, and are performing a new type of computation within the built environment. In these future scenarios, we have given them a whole new level of understanding and attention.

These living species are no longer on the sidelines, serving as aesthetics, food, or other human benefit — they are the main characters of our attentional practice.
